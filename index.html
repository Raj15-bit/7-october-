01

Money got dearer in India and abroad at the same moment

India's central bank raised its rate for the first time in more than 3 years, while long-term borrowing costs in America, Britain and Japan climbed to levels last seen around 2000.

In short
  • The Reserve Bank of India raised its main lending rate, its first rise since February 2023.
  • At the same time, the rate the US government pays to borrow for 10 years reached its highest since 2002.
  • So money is getting dearer for everyone, and the reason both times is the same: prices are rising faster than planned because of the war around Iran.

The single biggest change in the material is not a single event but the way 2 separate events lined up. India's central bank stopped waiting and raised rates. On the other side of the world, buyers of government bonds demanded much more interest to lend for long periods.

So the Indian economy is doing well, but it is importing costly energy and costly money from abroad. That is why its central bank acted even though growth is strong.

What the Reserve Bank of India actually did

The central bank sets the repo rate, which is the interest it charges banks for overnight loans. When it rises, loans across the country slowly get dearer. Governor Sanjay Malhotra said there will be no cuts soon, and the next move is either another rise or a pause.

  • Repo rate — raised by 0.25 percentage points to 5.50%, from 5.25%.
  • Stance — changed from "neutral" to "calibrated tightening", which means rates are more likely to go up than down.
  • Vote — all 6 members voted for the rise; the vote on the change of stance was 4 to 2.
  • Inflation forecast — raised to 5.2% for the year to March 2027, with 6.0% expected in the October-December quarter.
  • Growth forecast — kept strong at 7.1% for the same year.

The vote was reported 2 ways in the posts, as split and as unanimous. Both are right: the rate rise itself was unanimous, while the change of stance was split. A separate post gave a much lower inflation forecast; that figure turned out to be core inflation, not the full forecast.

Why the bank moved when the economy is strong

Inflation in India has risen for 10 months in a row and reached 4.8% in August, above the 4% target. The bank listed what worries it: crude oil, rising bond yields abroad, the war in West Asia, a weak monsoon and El Niño, a warm-water pattern in the Pacific that often brings poor rain to India.

There is also a quieter reason. Indian government bonds used to pay much more than American ones, which kept foreign money interested. That gap has been shrinking as American yields rose, so the rupee has been sliding. A higher Indian rate helps hold the gap open.

The rest of the world pulled the same way

A yield is the yearly return a lender asks for. When yields on long government bonds rise, every other long loan, from mortgages to company bonds, tends to get dearer too. Long yields rose across most rich countries together.

Is this the start of a long run of rises?

Views split. One view is that this rise is mostly about keeping the rupee steady and that India has too much cash in its banking system for a long tightening cycle. Bank cash was in surplus by about ₹5.9 lakh crore on average, so the hike mainly raises what banks earn on spare cash. The opposite view is that the bond market is already betting on about 1 percentage point more of rises within a year, and that oil and food prices will force the bank's hand.

Section 03 lays out the forecasts from each bank economist. The plain reading is that the first rise is done and the second depends on oil.

How it all connects

The themes in the rest of this issue are links in 1 chain. War raises shipping and fuel costs, those costs raise inflation, inflation raises interest rates, and higher rates hit the shares and loans that depended on cheap money.

02

The hard limits: ships, coal, water and skilled hands

Physical limits that money cannot quickly fix: a narrow sea lane under attack, power plants low on coal, a dry monsoon and too few trained people for data centres.

In short
  • Attacks on oil tankers near Iran reached their highest weekly count since the war began.
  • Many Indian power plants hold only a few days of coal, and the monsoon was weak.
  • India's data-centre boom needs far more trained engineers than it has.

The sea lane that carries the world's oil

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow channel between Iran and Oman through which most Gulf oil normally sails. Before the war, about half of India's crude imports and 9 in 10 of its LPG cargoes came this way. The war around Iran began on 28 February and is now in its 8th month.

The risk now is not that the strait is shut but that it is dangerous. Ships still pass, but crews must be paid danger money and insurers charge more, and those costs land in the price of every barrel.

How much oil is really getting out

Views split sharply on this. One view, from US officials and Goldman Sachs, is that Gulf oil exports are back to about 23.3 million barrels a day, the level before the war, partly through "dark" exports that switch off their tracking signals. The opposite view, from Standard Chartered, is that only about 60% of Gulf crude now goes through Hormuz, against 83% before the war, so the system is coping rather than healed.

  • Iran — loaded no crude at all in September, against about 1.7 million barrels a day before the war; US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Iran had no oil on the water for a whole week, a first.
  • Saudi Arabia — exports bounced to about 6.9 million barrels a day after its East-West pipeline was damaged in early September.
  • Ship-to-ship transfers — waits of 7 to 10 days, and volumes of about 12 million barrels a day, double a month earlier.
  • Iran's terms — its parliament speaker says the strait will not fully reopen until the US meets 7 conditions; Iran's forces say they will block "illegal routes" used by small boats.
  • Iran at home — President Masoud Pezeshkian asked people to ration electricity, gas, petrol and water.

The way to settle the argument is to watch freight rates. If the strait were truly normal, the cost of hiring a tanker would fall. Instead every crude freight index is at a record, which section 04 shows.

The war is spreading to other seas

Drones hit 2 ships inside Bulgaria's sea zone in the Black Sea, the first such attack inside a NATO member's waters. A ship sank. Indian sailors keep turning up in these stories, because Indian crews are a large share of the world's merchant seafarers.

  • Bulgaria — the Togo-flagged Alfa Watan sank; all 18 crew of the Able, including 7 Indians, were rescued.
  • Off Sochi — a sanctioned tanker carrying about 760,000 barrels of Russian crude bound for India caught fire; a separate drone strike hit a Liberian tanker whose 23 Indian crew are safe.
  • Saudi Arabia — Houthi forces from Yemen struck the airports at Jazan and Najran; Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey declared their "Mecca Defense Pact" in force.
  • Russia — military spending is set at a record of about $199 billion for 2026, roughly 40% above plan, while Ukraine keeps striking Russian refineries.

India's power plants are running low on coal

Electricity demand in India rose fast because the monsoon was weak, which cut hydropower and pushed up use of fans and air conditioners. Coal-fired plants carried the load and ate into their stocks. Rajasthan, Gujarat, Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu have asked for extra power.

A dry year is now visible in the cities

El Niño weakened the monsoon. Maharashtra, India's richest state, will cut home water supply by at least 10% after its weakest monsoon in more than a decade, and has told factories to recycle at least 30% of their water.

  • Food — retail prices of toor dal rose from ₹126 to ₹143 a kilogram in a month; sugar from ₹43 to ₹50.
  • Farm chemicals — makers of crop-protection products are expected to report sales down about 6% and profit down about 44% for July-September, because of poor rain and returned stock.
  • Government — cut import taxes on edible oil and allowed duty-free raw sugar imports.
  • Weather abroad — September ended without an Atlantic hurricane for the first time in 32 years, because El Niño winds tear storms apart.

So the dry year hits twice: it pushes food inflation up, which keeps the central bank cautious, and it cuts rural incomes, which matters for the farm-linked companies in section 07.

Data centres need a different kind of worker

A data centre built for artificial intelligence is close to a different building. An AI server rack draws roughly 10 to 15 times the power of an ordinary rack. That needs far more power and cooling, and people who know how to run them.

Only about 30,000 people work in Indian data centres. Taggd, a hiring firm, says about 270,000 more could be trained and another 220,000 have related skills. The government may invite Taiwan's ITRI, Singapore's A*STAR and Germany's Fraunhofer institutes to train chip engineers in India, which shows the same gap in semiconductors.

The common thread of this section is that the bottleneck is physical. Money can be raised in a week; ships, coal, rain and trained engineers cannot.

03

The price of money: who pays more, and who earns more

How far Indian rates may rise, which banks gain from it, why the rupee is at a record low, and why America's bond market is paying the highest rates in a generation.

In short
  • Traders are betting on about 1 percentage point more of rate rises in India within a year.
  • Large private banks gain, because most of their loans reprice quickly when rates rise.
  • In America, buyers still turned up for government bonds, but only at the highest yields since 2000.

What the bond market expects next

An overnight index swap, or OIS, is a contract that lets traders bet on where the overnight rate will be. The gap between the 1-year OIS rate and the repo rate shows how many more rises traders expect. That gap is about 1.01 percentage points, so traders expect about 4 more small rises within a year.

  • Peak fear — the gap reached about 1.12 points in April, when the US-Iran fighting was at its worst.
  • Calm spell — it fell to about 0.48 after the August meeting, when only 2 rises were expected.
  • Now — back near 1.01, the highest in more than 4 months.

Economists do not all agree with the traders. Their forecasts range from 1 more rise to 4 more.

When the repo rate rises, banks whose loans are tied to it charge more almost at once. An external benchmark lending rate, or EBLR, is a loan rate linked directly to the repo. Private banks have tied almost all their floating loans to it; public-sector banks still use an older, slower benchmark for much of their book.

Short rates are catching up with long rates

Normally a 10-year loan pays clearly more than a 5-year loan. Over September, shorter Indian government yields rose faster than longer ones, so the gap between them nearly halved. This flattening happens when markets expect the central bank to keep raising short-term rates.

  • 5-year yield — up 0.41 points in September to about 7.0%.
  • 10-year yield — up 0.27 points to about 7.25%, its highest in 33 months.
  • The gap — shrank from 0.44 to about 0.23 points.

The rupee at a record low

The rupee broke its May low and traded near 97 per dollar, its weakest ever, after losing about 7% this year. Foreign selling and a wide trade gap push it down: foreign investors pulled about $10.3 billion out of India from April.

One view is that a weak rupee is an honest price for an economy that imports its energy, and it helps exporters such as software companies, whose rupee earnings rise about 8.7% from the currency alone. The opposite view is that a falling rupee scares foreign investors further, which forces more selling and a weaker rupee. Motilal Oswal expects about 96.40 per dollar, a little stronger than now.

America's bond market: buyers came, but at a price

The US Federal Reserve raised rates in September, and the minutes show all 19 officials backed it. Markets give about an 80% chance of another rise in December. The Fed chair is now Kevin Warsh. President Trump said rates should come down, but called Warsh "one vote".

Primary dealers are big banks that must bid at every Treasury auction. When they take only 2.5% of a sale, it means investors and foreign central banks bought nearly all of it. So high yields brought demand, but at a cost to every borrower who prices off them.

  • 3-year auction — sold at 4.932%, the highest since 2006, with foreign demand at its lowest since February.
  • Homebuyers — the monthly payment on a $400,000 loan is about $370 higher than before the war; mortgage applications are down about 50% since January.
  • Company bonds — the average yield on high-grade company debt is 6.51%, the highest since 2004.

2 opposite stories about why yields are high

One view is that the world has moved from too much savings to too little: governments, AI builders and war all need money at once, so borrowers should lock in long loans now. Bank of America's call for 3 more Fed rises fits this story. The opposite view is that governments with record debt will let inflation run, as after the Second World War, when inflation shrank US debt from about 106% of GDP to about 23%. On that reading, long bonds are a poor place to hold savings.

Gold fell even as buyers kept coming

Gold traded near $4,121 an ounce, a 2-month low and about 12% below its late-summer peak. Yet gold funds kept their holdings near record levels, and central banks keep buying.

  • China's central bank — added 20 tonnes in August, its 22nd straight month of buying, to reach 2,345 tonnes.
  • Goldman Sachs — estimates central banks buy about 91 tonnes a month, against 17 before 2022; it still sees $5,400 by end-2027.
  • Producers — Indonesia, Laos and others now refine and keep their own gold rather than export it.

So gold is being pulled 2 ways: high real interest rates make it less attractive to traders, while central banks moving away from the dollar keep a floor under it.

04

Oil, diesel and freight: the barrel is not the whole bill

Crude near $100 hides the real cost. Shipping, diesel and gas are where the strain shows, and a few chemicals that India makes are suddenly worth more.

In short
  • Hiring a large oil tanker now costs about 21 times what it did a year ago.
  • Europe is short of diesel and gas going into winter.
  • Indian airlines and other heavy fuel users are paying the bill, while some chemical makers gain.

The price of crude, 3 ways

Brent is the main world oil price. Dated Brent is the price for cargoes loading soon, and it includes the cost of getting them. The gap between the 2 shows how much the war is adding.

Views split on where this goes. One view is that the price is held down only by emergency releases from the rich world, so the fall is temporary; the head of trader Vitol says 10 to 14 million barrels a day from the Middle East are vital, or oil could reach $200. The opposite view is that Gulf exports are already back to pre-war levels, so once freight cools, crude should drift down.

  • Emergency oil — G7 countries agreed to release up to 100 million barrels; the US has released 132.5 million since late March.
  • US reserve — the Strategic Petroleum Reserve fell to 283 million barrels, only 13 million above its record low.
  • US output — near a record 14 million barrels a day, but barely higher than a year ago despite $90 oil.
  • Iraq — devalued its currency, the dinar, by about 13% to 1,520 per dollar, because its oil exports have fallen and oil is about 90% of state revenue.
  • Venezuela — the 310,000 barrel-a-day Cardón refinery stopped after a fire; India's imports of Venezuelan crude are set to reach about 4.65 lakh barrels a day in October, the most since December 2019.
  • Ecuador — is in talks to sell more crude, copper and critical minerals to India.

Ships are the new bottleneck

A VLCC, or very large crude carrier, carries about 2 million barrels. Its daily hire rate on the Saudi Arabia to China run hit a record $1.33 million, about 21 times the rate a year ago. Freight now makes up about a quarter of the delivered cost of crude, up from almost nothing at the start of the year.

The rise is not only on Gulf routes. Tankers from the US Gulf, West Africa and the Black Sea also hit records, because ships that take long detours are tied up longer, which leaves fewer ships for everyone else. In June, rates nearly halved within days after a spike, so the record can reverse quickly if the strait calms.

So the winners are tanker owners, which is why shipping companies are listing their shares: the tanker arm of trader Trafigura jumped on its debut in Oslo. The losers are refiners and countries far from their crude.

Diesel and gas in Europe

Diesel moves trucks, ships, farm machines and generators, so its price leaks into almost everything. Europe has closed about 30 refineries since 2009, and Russia and China have both stopped selling fuel abroad.

  • Price — European diesel was about $186 a barrel, up 72% in a year.
  • Margin — the profit from turning crude into diesel is about $90 a barrel over Brent, according to Bank of America.
  • France — will release 10 million barrels of diesel from its reserves.
  • United States — exported a seasonal record 1.76 million barrels a day of diesel; East Coast stocks are at record lows; households will pay about 21% more for heating oil this winter.

Gas is the other weak point. On 1 October, Europe's gas stores were only 71.5% full, the lowest in records going back to 2011.

Indian airlines and fuel users pay the bill

Jet fuel in Delhi rose by ₹16 a litre to about ₹137 from 1 October. The Federation of Indian Airlines says fuel is now more than half of airline running costs, far above normal years. Because fuel is priced in dollars, the weak rupee makes it worse.

The government is thinking of reviving a price-smoothing scheme for jet fuel; an earlier ₹10,000 crore scheme lapsed. Domestic air passengers have stayed flat at about 16.7 crore a year since mid-2025. So airlines face high costs and no growth in passengers, which is the worst mix for a business with high fixed costs.

05

India's policy moves, and how the market priced them

New rules for chips, cars, insurance and exports, a fund for small firms, and an old fight over whether India's steel needs so much protection.

In short
  • India will pay chip-makers only if they buy materials and parts locally.
  • New car-emission rules ended a special break that helped small cars.
  • A change to insurance sales rules wiped out about half the value of 2 online insurance sellers in a month.

Chips: subsidy only with local sourcing

The India Semiconductor Mission is the government programme that pays part of the cost of chip plants. Its second phase, ISM 2.0, has a budget of ₹1.27 lakh crore. The ministry of electronics has told applicants they must source raw materials, gases, chemicals and machines from Indian suppliers where possible, backed by signed declarations, or payments will stop.

Is the production-linked subsidy the right tool?

A production-linked incentive, or PLI, pays companies a share of extra sales they make in India. Views split on it. One view is that it pays for output rather than building real supply chains; in telecom, about ₹6,000 to 7,000 crore of PLI money compares with about ₹10,000 crore that a simple subsidy on plant and power would have cost. The opposite view points to penicillin G, a basic antibiotic input that India stopped making after Chinese price cuts.

  • The collapse — Torrent stopped making penicillin G in 2005; Alembic and SPIC shut their plants by 2012.
  • The restart — Aurobindo Pharma restarted at Kakinada in 2024 with PLI help and makes about 9,000 to 10,000 tonnes a year, close to India's whole need.
  • China's reply — cut its price by 40% to 50% in 2025; India answered with minimum import prices on penicillin G, 6-APA and amoxicillin.
  • America — a US study found its amoxicillin depends on 4 starting materials made in China.

So the strongest case for PLI is a narrow 1: bulk drugs where China can undercut any newcomer, and where a minimum price is also needed for the plant to survive.

Maruti Suzuki wanted a 3 gram credit for very light cars; Tata Motors and Mahindra opposed it, the fight reached the Prime Minister's Office, and the credit was dropped. Power and the pushback

Data centres need electricity that grids cannot supply quickly. So tech companies now pay to build or reserve their own power.

  • Finland — ordered Google to halt 2 data centres after more than 300 hectares of forest were cleared without a review.

GigawattA million kilowatts, about the output of 1 large power plant.

Main board and SME platformThe main board lists larger companies; the SME platform lists small companies under lighter rules.
Margin trading facilityA broker loan that lets an investor buy shares with partly borrowed money.
